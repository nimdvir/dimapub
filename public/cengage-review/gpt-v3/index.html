The proposition

What is this book about?

Courseware for undergraduate business students that connects the way data is organized, queried and managed to the decisions an organization makes.

In this course, students learn how to understand data, organize it, store it in a relational database, ask questions of it in SQL, check the answers, analyze and visualize the results in Power BI, and make a decision. AI appears throughout as an assistant whose work students learn to check.

Textbook

A textbook, interactive reader, guided practice, independent labs, reading assessments, and a semester project are designed to work as one course. The proposed next edition has 16 chapters in five parts.

The author’s starting point

Why and how it was built

I wrote this book because, after more than six years of teaching this course to over 4,000 students with materials from all the leading publishers, I still could not find a textbook that matched my needs.

Available textbooks were either too technical, too thin on hands-on practice, or missing crucial content. As cloud databases, analytics and visualization tools, and AI entered the workplace, the gap became stark. I often assigned at least two books and filled the rest of the gaps myself.

The market and curriculum research below helped define a focused opportunity: courseware that brings database foundations, hands-on work and business interpretation together.

Based on my experience and on the research, I concluded that the content, the tools and the speed of revision all had to change. So I built the courseware myself, chapter by chapter, over a year of work with colleagues, and tested it live in the classroom.

One SQL foundation

Technology and tools

The syllabus platform audit distinguishes documented practical use, a named option, and a software-list mention. Counts use 54 selected course families and overlap across tools. Official documentation then informs the proposed PGlite, Supabase, and Power BI pathway. [S2] [T1] [T2] [T3]

Practical use means the reviewed text assigns or demonstrates work with the tool; an option does not establish that a student selected it. Twenty course families lack sufficiently explicit practical / listed platform evidence. No adoption of Supabase or PGlite was established in this archive.

Main conclusion

MySQL and Access have substantial documented footprints in the reviewed courses. Keep meaningful compatibility routes while using one principal SQL foundation. The choice of PostgreSQL is a design recommendation, rather than the dominant platform in this sample.

The proposed technology pathway

Recommendation: PostgreSQL as the SQL foundation, PGlite for immediate practice in the browser, and Supabase for the hosted project. Power BI provides the main reporting studio; Tableau is an alternate route. Lucidchart supports modeling, with a supported alternative. Access and MySQL receive substantive companion coverage.

Assessment principle

Use known-result SQL and integrity tests where they fit; use human review for modeling, explanations, and business judgment. Evaluate unfamiliar variations and require a short individual explanation. AI-assisted feedback is a development option that needs validation.

One problem. Three checkpoints. A defensible recommendation.

  1. Question and requirements. A scoped business problem, stakeholder, measure, source plan and acceptance criteria. Start in the opening chapters.
  2. Design and working data. An ERD, normalization rationale, implemented schema, synthetic data, import/reject log and integrity checks.
  3. Evidence and action. Tested SQL, reconciled reporting, essential access/recovery controls, a decision memo and an individual change task.

A clear courseware proposition

How it is different

This book differentiates itself by teaching business students to use data to make decisions. Therefore, it differs from the main textbooks mainly by:

  • Starting with the business. Chapter 1 opens with performance, goals and KPIs, before any database theory.
  • Teaching data before databases. Data types, measurement scales and organizing information come first.
  • Using a database before designing one. Students query a supplied database in Chapters 4 to 6, then design their own.
  • Ending with the decision. Analytics, dashboards and a full Power BI chapter lead to a decision memo.
  • One SQL dialect, flexible tools. SQL is taught in the PostgreSQL dialect, with a portable core and explicit dialect differences, with planned, tested MySQL and Access routes on the same cases.
  • Industry tools students can keep. Supabase provides a hosted PostgreSQL project; Power BI supplies a reporting studio, with Tableau as an alternate route. Account limits, licensing and classroom access need an agreed support plan.
  • Making AI a habit. Students practice accepting, revising or rejecting AI-assisted work, with responsibility for checking the result.
  • Practice in every chapter. A Let's Build, an interactive lab and an auto-graded quiz, all on the book's own cases.
  • Developed in class. Version 1 was tested in summer 2026; version 2 is in use with 500 students this fall.
