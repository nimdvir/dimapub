Overview

The book, in brief

Courseware about the relationship between data and business performance.

I want students to see how the pieces fit together: understand the data, build a reliable database, ask a useful question in SQL, check the answer, and turn it into a recommendation.

A database course that feels like business.

The goal is a complete journey from data to a decision students can explain. The database foundations stay rigorous; the business question gives them a purpose.

The opportunity

A complete teaching package already in use, with a research-informed plan for its next edition. Editorial development and instructor review can help bring it to more classrooms.

Who is it for?

Primary course
The applied business database or MIS data-management course: modeling, normalization, SQL through window functions, administration, warehousing and business intelligence, taught through business cases.
Business core
The required data or information systems course for accounting, finance, marketing, management and operations students, who need to query, check and present data. A shorter route through the same chapters serves it.
Adjacent courses
Accounting information systems, business analytics foundations, and bridge courses in IS master's programs.

Development

Why and how it was built

After seven years of teaching this course to more than 4,000 students, I had a familiar problem: I liked parts of several textbooks, but no single book brought together everything I wanted students to learn. I often assigned two books and wrote the missing pieces myself.

So I began building the courseware I wanted to teach. Over a year of writing and work with colleagues, it became a complete course, tested and revised in summer and fall 2026.

The classroom gave me the starting point. The research helped me question and refine it. I changed the material after teaching it, tested the revisions in class, and continue to revise where students need more support.

What informed the design

  • Teaching: seven years of seeing where business students need more context, practice or confidence.
  • Editing: more than ten years as a magazine editor, with an emphasis on clear, visual writing.
  • Research: 63 course documents from 31 institutions, a 60-title catalogue, detailed textbook comparisons, and market and employer evidence.
  • Iteration: a platform I can revise as tools change and students tell me what needs attention.

Ready now

  • The full textbook, live as an interactive digital edition and in use at the University at Albany
  • A Let's Build, an interactive lab and an auto-graded quiz in every chapter
  • Student accounts, automatic quiz grading and an instructor dashboard
  • Two running cases: the Grading Database and PetVax
  • An Access route: Access labs and an Access appendix

Why
To check the outline against what courses across the country actually teach: which topics, which tools, which textbooks, how they assess, and what they say about AI.
What we collected
63 syllabi and course documents: syllabi, catalog descriptions and course schedules for database and business-data courses, mostly undergraduate, from the public websites of 31 universities. Retrieved October 1, 2026.
Why 63 documents but 31 institutions
Many institutions contribute more than one course, and some courses have more than one document. The 63 documents describe 54 distinct courses at 31 institutions. Of the 54 courses, 19 are business-database or business-core data courses, the closest match to this book.
How we read them
Archived documents and the earlier audits were reviewed for topics, platforms, textbooks, assessment and AI policies. Each set of findings retains its own sample and denominator. [S1]

Where they stop, and this book continues

  • The four compared chapter sequences open with database foundations rather than a three-chapter business-performance, data-literacy and information-organization sequence. This book does, in Chapters 1 to 3.
  • A dedicated Power BI studio is a proposed distinction in this comparison, alongside analytics, visual reporting and a capstone decision memo. Current competing texts already cover aspects of BI and analytics.
  • Competing texts cover AI as a topic; Modern Database Management, for example, adds State of AI sections. This design makes checking AI-assisted work a recurring practice, with structured verification activities from Chapter 5 onward.
  • Courses and books support different database products. This proposal keeps PostgreSQL central, keeps the existing Access route, and plans a MySQL route to be tested on the same cases.

Research 05

Classroom development and student feedback

The book has been taught, changed, and tested again in class, rather than developed only as a manuscript.

“I understand the terms but not how they connect.”

The classroom problem this book is designed to address.

The syntax is the easy part.

The harder part is knowing which question to ask, finding the right data, and trusting the answer enough to put your name on a recommendation. That is the connection I want the book to make.

I write the way I teach: explaining why a step matters, showing how it works, and giving students a chance to try it. I revise the material where students need more help.

  • Connect the pieces: keep the business question visible as students move from data to SQL, reports and decisions.
  • Make practice predictable: pair a guided Let’s Build with an independent lab and a reading check.

Assessment principle

Use known-result SQL and integrity tests where they fit; use human review for modeling, explanations, and business judgment. Evaluate unfamiliar variations and require a short individual explanation. Automated feedback on SQL and lab answers is a prototype in testing, and AI-assisted feedback is a development option that needs validation.

One problem. Three checkpoints. A defensible recommendation.

  1. Question and requirements. A scoped business problem, stakeholder, measure, source plan and acceptance criteria. Start in the opening chapters.
  2. Design and working data. An ERD, normalization rationale, implemented schema, synthetic data, import/reject log and integrity checks.
  3. Evidence and action. Tested SQL, reconciled reporting, essential access/recovery controls, a decision memo and an individual change task.
