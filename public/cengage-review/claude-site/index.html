This is the prospectus for a courseware that includes a textbook, an interactive reader, labs, quizzes, guided practice and a semester project. What is this book about?

The book is aimed at mandatory courses in business programs across the United States that focus on the relationship between data and business decisions.

In this course, students learn how to understand data, organize it, store it in a relational database, ask questions of it in SQL, check the answers, analyze and visualize the results in Power BI, and make a decision. AI appears throughout as an assistant whose work students learn to check.

The course arc

Data→Tables→Relationships→Queries→Analytics→Decisions

Textbook

The proposed outline has 16 chapters in five parts. Every chapter follows the same structure:

  1. Introduction. The business question and the chapter objectives.
  2. Core concepts. The main chapter, in numbered sections with worked examples.
  3. Terms Treasury. The key terms, defined in context.
  4. Review questions. Recall and reflection.
  5. Reading Assessment Test. An auto-graded quiz, tied to the book's own databases.
  6. Let's Build. Guided, hands-on practice on the running case.
  7. Interactive lab. Independent practice on a second case, with hints and an AI Check.
  8. Key takeaways. A summary and a bridge to the next chapter.
  9. Semester project. Three graded checkpoints across the book, ending in a capstone decision memo.

Why and how it was built

I wrote this book because, after more than six years of teaching this course to over 4,000 students with materials from all the leading publishers, I still could not find a textbook that matched my needs.

Available textbooks were either too technical, too thin on hands-on practice, or missing crucial content. As cloud databases, analytics and visualization tools, and AI entered the workplace, the gap became stark. I often assigned at least two books and filled the rest of the gaps myself.

I also saw a strong market opportunity. The course is required in most business programs, no single textbook dominates it, and the applied titles that serve it are several years old. The market research below gives the figures.

Based on my experience and on the research, I concluded that the content, the tools and the speed of revision all had to change. So I built the courseware myself, chapter by chapter, over a year of work with colleagues, and tested it live in the classroom.

Things that shaped the structure of this courseware

Seven years in the classroom
I have taught the introductory MIS course at the University at Albany for seven years, to more than 4,000 students. The chapter labs and quizzes were developed and refined across those semesters.
More than ten years of editing
Before I became a professor, I spent over ten years as a magazine editor. That shaped the writing: clear, visual and organized around the reader, at a level business undergraduates can follow.
Extensive market research
I reviewed and analyzed the main textbooks used in these courses, surveyed what universities across the country teach, and compared 63 syllabi collected from 31 institutions. The outline draws directly on that work. See the research below.
Full control and quick iteration
Because I built the platform, tools, examples and AI guidance can change each semester instead of each edition. After the summer test and the current deployment with 500 students, I collect live feedback from students and revise continuously.

Running cases

Every Let's Build uses one guided case and every lab uses a second case, so students learn a skill once and then transfer it. The case choice is one of the open questions.

Guided case

Grading database

Every student understands courses, assignments and grades, so no class time goes to explaining the business.

What to test: Keep as the guided case with a small, stable schema.

Lab case

PetVax veterinary clinic

What to test: Whether students relate to the setting.

To check the outline against what courses across the country actually teach: which topics, which tools, which textbooks, how they assess, and what they say about AI.
What we collected
63 syllabi and course documents: syllabi, catalog descriptions and course schedules for database and business-data courses, mostly undergraduate, from the public websites of 31 universities. Retrieved October 1, 2026.
Why 63 documents but 31 institutions
Many institutions contribute more than one course, and some courses have more than one document. The 63 documents describe 54 distinct courses at 31 institutions. Of the 54 courses, 19 are business-database or business-core data courses, the closest match to this book.
How we read them
Every document was read and coded by hand for topics, platforms, textbooks, assessment and AI policies.

How they assess, and what they say about AI

Assessment
Most courses rely on exams and a term project. Many run staged, multi-phase projects, often in teams. Structured hands-on practice with automatic feedback in every week is rare, which leaves students with few chances to practice before they are graded.
AI
Only 12 of the syllabi include any reference to using AI in the course. The rest say nothing about it.

What it tells us: every book shares the same relational core, which this book keeps. All four open with database foundations, teach SQL by Chapter 3 to 5, and end in operations or a short analytics chapter. None opens with business and data literacy, and none ends with a full reporting chapter and a capstone.

What the leading books do well, and this book keeps

  • The relational core: keys, relationships, ER modeling and normalization to third normal form
  • Early, hands-on SQL, as in Coronel 15th edition, Kroenke and zyBooks
  • Administration, transactions and security as a full chapter
  • Warehousing, star schemas and NoSQL as the bridge to analytics

Where they stop, and this book continues

  • None of the four main texts opens with business performance, data types and organizing information. This book does, in Chapters 1 to 3.
  • None has a Power BI chapter, so the course ends before the dashboard and the decision. This book ends with analytics, reporting, Power BI and a decision memo.
  • AI appears as a topic, not as a skill practiced in every chapter. This book has an AI Check in every chapter.
  • Most center on one database product. This book teaches one SQL dialect with tested routes for the tools courses already use.

How it is different

This book differentiates itself by teaching business students to use data to make decisions. Therefore, it differs from the main textbooks mainly by:

  • Starting with the business. Chapter 1 opens with performance, goals and KPIs, before any database theory.
  • Teaching data before databases. Data types, measurement scales and organizing information come first.
  • Using a database before designing one. Students query a supplied database in Chapters 4 to 6, then design their own.
  • Ending with the decision. Analytics, dashboards and a full Power BI chapter lead to a decision memo.
  • One SQL dialect, flexible tools. SQL is taught in the PostgreSQL dialect, which transfers to every system, with tested MySQL and Access routes on the same cases.
  • Industry tools students can keep. Supabase is a free cloud database that anyone can use, and it is widely used in industry. Power BI is the reporting tool employers ask for.
  • Making AI a habit. Every chapter has an AI Check: accept, revise or reject AI-written work.
  • Practice in every chapter. A Let's Build, an interactive lab and an auto-graded quiz, all on the book's own cases.
  • Proven in class. Version 1 was tested in summer 2026; version 2 is in use with 500 students this fall.

Advantages of this courseware

The courseware already works in the classroom, and each chapter ties reading, guided practice, a lab and an auto-graded quiz to the same business cases.

Ready asset
Live, in its second version, and used by 500 students this semester. The textbook, labs, quizzes and instructor materials exist today, so the work ahead is revision and review, not a first draft.
Two markets
One book serves the business-core data course and the MIS database course through two routes, from the same chapters.
Open slot
No textbook dominates this course, and the applied titles that serve it are several years old. This book can serve that course with current SQL, analytics and AI, and complement the deeper texts rather than compete with them.
Known partner
I already work with Cengage as a member of its Intro Computing Thought Leadership Board, and I have taught this course for seven years.
Additional opportunities
Adjacent courses such as accounting information systems and business analytics foundations, custom editions, and MySQL and Access routes that let current adopters keep the labs they already run.

Open questions

If and when we decide to move forward, there are still some open questions that should be discussed:

  1. 1

    Positioning and content

    Review the suggested outline together: what to add, where to complement the current books, and where not to compete, and plan the content from there.

  2. 2

    Tools and access

    Which application routes should be adopted, and which SQL platforms should be fully supported: MS Access, MySQL, PostgreSQL. Power BI for reporting.

  3. 3

    Platform fit

    How the chapter package (reading assessments, labs, auto-graded SQL) maps to MindTap, and whether a live SQL environment such as Supabase or in-browser PostgreSQL can connect to it.

  4. 4

    Running cases

    The current cases are a grading database and a veterinary clinic. Would reviewers prefer a commercial case, such as retail orders and revenue?

  5. 5

    Review package

    What should be included in the review: this outline, two or three sample chapters, a lab, reader access? How many reviewers, and with what criteria?
